## 1. What this source is

The **speaker script** for a clinical-documentation / revenue-cycle session (RCM = revenue cycle management). It teaches clinicians how documentation, ICD-10 coding and CCHI/insurer policy determine whether a claim is approved, and how to avoid the most common rejection causes.

Its core message, as the script puts it:

> *"Better documentation = Better patient care + Better revenue integrity."*
> *"Your documentation is your strongest defence and your strongest revenue tool."*
> *"Clinical decision + Documentation + Coding = optimised payment."*

It pairs with the 2026 slide deck (file 06) — the slide deck has the same topics, but the script adds the delivery notes and detail.

### Learning objectives (from Slide 2)
1. Explain how documentation strategy affects claim acceptance and approval rate.
2. Select the right ICD-10 code to obtain pre-authorisation at the first attempt.
3. Identify what to document and what to avoid.
4. Understand CCHI regulations (the governing body for the RCM cycle, as described).
5. Improve clinical clarity → coding accuracy → billing outcomes.

## 2. Slide-by-slide content

### Slide 1 — Introduction
- Opening: CDI (Clinical Documentation Improvement) is about reducing rejections *and* making sure clinical decisions are accurately, justifiably and payably recorded.
- Stated goal: **better documentation = better patient care + better revenue integrity.**
- Direction for the future: a unified medical file for every patient across all providers, so claims quality follows one documentation strategy.

### Slide 2 — Objectives
- Five objectives (listed in Section 1 above).
- Key message: *"Your documentation is your strongest defence and your strongest revenue tool."* Also: *"Give the patient what he needs, not what he wants."*

### Documentation Strategy (CDI Core)
- Principle: **every service must be clinically justified.**
- **Always:** clearly document chief complaint and HPI · highlight relevant clinical findings · link symptoms to the working diagnosis · support the diagnosis with labs/imaging when needed.
- **Avoid:** vague or incomplete notes · diagnosis without clinical support · copy-paste without clinical relevance · invalid medical data such as "fatigue only" or "body pain" on their own.

### Medical Necessity
- "Medical necessity is the core driver of approval." Every medication, lab or procedure needs a clear indication.
- The payer asks: *Why was this done? Was it clinically justified?*
- **Example (same treatment, different outcome):**
  - Weak: *"Patient given IV fluids."*
  - Strong: *"Patient presented with dehydration signs (tachycardia, dry mucosa); IV fluids started for stabilisation."*

### Full History & Clinical Picture
- To get a professional diagnosis first time, build a complete picture:
  - Chief complaint · detailed history of present illness · relevant past history · clinical findings · assessment · plan with duration and follow-up.
- "Good documentation tells a story — not just a diagnosis."

### ICD-10 Coding (Core Concept)
- "Coding is not administrative — it's clinical translation."
- The **primary diagnosis** reflects the main condition treated; **secondary diagnoses** show complexity.
- **Specific codes = higher acceptance**, and they justify medications and other services.
- **Biggest single cause of rejection: non-specific coding of symptoms.** Examples to avoid and their specific alternatives:

| Avoid (symptom code) | Use instead (specific diagnosis) |
|---|---|
| Fever (R50) | — (identify the cause) |
| Cough (R05) | — (identify the cause) |
| Vomiting (R11) | Gastroenteritis (K52) |
| Sore throat | Acute tonsillitis (J03) |
| Headache | Hypertension (I10), when clinically supported |

- **Key rule:** don't stop at symptoms — reach the most supported clinical diagnosis.
- **Golden tip:** if you requested investigations based on suspicion, you may document a **provisional diagnosis**, as long as it is clinically justified.

### Professional Diagnosis
To achieve a professional diagnosis on the first submission:
- Base the diagnosis on clinical findings.
- Support it with labs/imaging when needed.
- Document your clinical reasoning.
- Use a provisional diagnosis if not yet confirmed.
- Specify the condition.
- Confirm whether the patient is already on treatment.
- Avoid uncovered ICD-10 codes (next section).

### Avoid Uncovered ICD-10 Codes
- Diagnoses commonly **not covered** by the policy, which should not be used as the primary diagnosis:
  - Obesity (E66) · Acne (L70) · Hair loss (L66) · Seborrhoeic dermatitis (L21) · Male or female infertility (N46, N97) · all general check-up codes — unless clearly linked to a covered medical condition.
- Options: link the condition to a covered condition when possible, or do not use it as the main diagnosis.
- **Obesity nuance (as stated in the script):** obesity is uncovered for medical treatment, but if BMI is **above 40** it is covered and the patient can be referred to surgery.

### Avoid Defensive Medicine
- "Defensive medicine increases rejections." Avoid:
  - Ordering unnecessary tests.
  - Prescribing supplements without indication — **combined supplements are treated as defensive medicine.**
- Follow initial work-up protocols. *More tests ≠ better care.*
- **Worked example from the script:** a patient with multi-joint pain who receives CRP and ESR first, then specific labs (anti-DNA, anti-Ro, anti-La …) all in one go, risks rejection for early/unjustified requests. Mention the past history or the specialist's diagnosis (e.g. if rheumatoid arthritis is already diagnosed by another doctor) and the full lab panel can be requested in one request.

### Avoid Excessive Ordering (timing matters)
- Early repetition of tests leads to rejection. Examples given:
  - Repeating vitamin D too early — the script says "not less than 118" (**the figure is garbled in the source; confirm the interval before publishing**).
  - Repeating HbA1c before "89 day" (**garbled; the standard interval is about 3 months (90 days) — confirm**).
- Follow evidence-based intervals; justify any early repetition. *"Unjustified repetition = automatic rejection."*

### Common Documentation Errors
The most common causes of rejection:
1. Missing clinical justification.
2. Non-specific diagnosis.
3. Incomplete documentation.
4. Mismatch between diagnosis and treatment.

### CCHI Updates & Policy Awareness
- Insurance is not static: new benefits are added, existing benefits are amended, and coverage rules change frequently.
- As a physician you should: stay aware of amended benefits · be familiar with newly added services · understand coverage limitations and conditions.
- "Lack of awareness leads to unnecessary rejections — even if your clinical decision is correct — and missed value-based revenue."

### Added Benefits (mental health & more)
- One of the major recent expansions is **mental-health coverage** in many plans. Certain services are sub-limit benefits that require **pre-authorisation**; the script cites a **sub-limit of 50,000 SAR** for the policy term.
- Conditions the script lists as covered when clinically documented: anxiety disorders · depression · behavioural conditions · psychological consultations · post-traumatic stress · eating disorders (bulimia, anorexia nervosa).
- Document: the clinical indication; link symptoms to a valid psychiatric diagnosis; justify referrals and management plans.

### Amended Benefits & Coverage Expansion
- Some services previously rejected are now covered; some conditions require specific approval criteria; coverage depends on the table of benefits.
- Document severity, duration and impact on the patient. **Partial coverage does not mean full approval.**
- *"The same service may be accepted or rejected depending on how it is documented."*
- **Example from the script:** hormonal assessment for PCOS will be rejected as uncovered; hormonal assessment for menopausal conditions is approved, as is hormone-replacement therapy.

### Physician Responsibility in Insurance Awareness
- Insurance awareness is part of the clinical responsibility.
- Know what is covered and what is not; adapt documentation; align clinical decisions with coverage rules.
- "The payer does not see your patient. The payer only sees your documentation."
- Final highlight: *"If you don't document it properly, it will not be covered, even if it is clinically correct."*

### To Summarise (closing checklist)
1. ✔ Be specific; avoid uncovered ICD-10 codes and combined supplements.
2. ✔ Full medical data and a detailed management plan with duration and expected outcome.
3. ✔ Justify all services and medications with a specific ICD-10 code; write a provisional diagnosis if needed.
4. ✔ Think like a clinician *and* a reviewer.
5. If a protocol is not followed (initial work-up, chronic disease follow-up, antenatal care), document *why*.
6. Make it clear, strong and defensible.
