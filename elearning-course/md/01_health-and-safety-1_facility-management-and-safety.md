### Module 1 — CBAHI Facility Management & Safety Chapter Overview (pages 2–8)

**Page 2 — Module cover.** Objective: name each of the eight plans and describe what each exists to do. Plans listed: *Fire · Building · Hazmat & Waste · Utility · Biomedical · Security · Internal Disaster · External Disaster.*

**Page 3 — Why this chapter exists (Framing).**
- CBAHI requires every healthcare facility to show that its physical environment, equipment and emergency systems actively protect patients, staff and visitors.
- Facility Management & Safety is described as "not a back-office formality" — it is a **surveyed accreditation chapter**, built on eight interlocking plans covering every physical risk in the building.
- Three key points:
  1. **A survey requirement** — surveyors assess each of the eight plans through documentation, staff knowledge and physical evidence.
  2. **Everyone's responsibility** — plans only work if every employee, clinical or non-clinical, knows their role in each one.
  3. **One connected system** — the plans overlap in practice. *A fire is a Code Red; a chemical spill is a Code Orange.* The plans and the emergency codes are "two views of the same system."

### Module 2 — Occupational Health & Safety (pages 9–15)

**Page 9 — Module cover.** Objective: core OHS definitions, how to identify and control risk, who to contact in your department, and your rights under ISO 45001.

**Page 10 — Core definitions ("Foundations").**
- **Health** — a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, not merely the absence of injury or illness.
- **Safety** — freedom from conditions that can cause injury, illness or damage, achieved by controlling hazards *before* they cause harm.
- **Hazard** — anything with the potential to cause harm (a wet floor, an unlabelled chemical, an exposed wire, a violent individual).
- **Risk** — the likelihood that a hazard will actually cause harm, combined with how severe that harm would be.

**Page 11 — Incident types.**

| Type | Definition (as written) |
|---|---|
| Near miss | Nothing happened, but it easily could have — "the single most valuable incident type to report." |
| First aid case | Minor injury treated with on-site first aid; no further medical care required. |
| Medical treatment case | Injury requiring treatment beyond first aid, such as stitches or prescribed medication. |
| Lost time injury | Injury serious enough that the employee cannot return to normal duties for a period. |
| Fatality | Death resulting from a workplace incident; triggers mandatory investigation and regulatory reporting. |

**Page 12 — The AREC framework (risk management cycle).** Four stages, repeated continuously:
1. **Anticipate** — think ahead about what could go wrong before starting a task or entering an area.
2. **Recognise** — identify hazards as they actually appear in your environment or task.
3. **Evaluate** — assess how likely the hazard is to cause harm and how severe that harm would be.
4. **Control** — apply the Hierarchy of Controls, starting with the most effective option.
Closing line: *hazard awareness is never a one-time task.*

**Page 13 — Hierarchy of controls.** "Start at the top. The higher a control sits, the more reliably it removes the risk — PPE is the last line of defence, not the first."
1. **Elimination** — physically remove the hazard.
2. **Substitution** — replace it with something safer.
3. **Engineering controls** — isolate people from the hazard.
4. **Administrative controls** — change how people work.
5. **PPE** — protect the individual worker.

**Page 14 — Department Safety Liaisons.**
- Appointed by departments to facilitate safety information and comply with the Environmental Health & Safety (EHS) department.
- If you do not know your liaison, ask your direct supervisor (who can tell you on your first day).
- Role: first point of contact for hazard reporting, risk assessments and PPE questions; the bridge to EHS.

**Page 15 — Your rights under ISO 45001.** Framing: "these rights exist so that safety concerns are never silenced by fear of consequences."
1. **Right to stop unsafe work** — stop any task you believe could cause serious harm, without fear of retaliation.
2. **Right to be consulted** — if you are afraid to stop work yourself, you can raise the concern with your Safety Officer or supervisor.
3. **Right to training** — trained on hazards and safe procedures relevant to your role *before* you are expected to perform it.

*Note for editing:* the text for each right is duplicated in the PDF's text layer (visual overlay artefact). Use the single version above.

### Module 4 — Fire Safety Plan (pages 22–31)

**Page 22 — Module cover.** Objective: how fire behaves, how to respond using RACE and PASS, and what fire-prevention rules apply to everyone.

**Page 23 — The fire triangle and fire behaviour.**
- Three elements: **Heat** (ignition source), **Fuel** (combustible material), **Oxygen** (sustains combustion). Remove any one and the fire cannot start or continue — "the logic behind every fire control method."
- Fire behaviour: a fire starts small and grows exponentially as heat radiates and ignites nearby fuel. **Smoke, not flame, is the leading cause of death in facility fires**, and spreads faster than fire through corridors and duct systems.
- Therefore RACE prioritises rescue and containment *before* extinguishing.

**Page 24 — Fire response: RACE and PASS.**

*RACE — if you discover a fire:*
1. **Rescue** — move anyone in immediate danger away from the fire.
2. **Alarm** — activate the nearest fire alarm pull station and call switchboard/security. State the **code type, your location, and your ID & name**.
3. **Contain** — remove flammables such as oxygen cylinders and alcohol-based sanitisers; close doors and windows to slow smoke and fire.
4. **Extinguish or Evacuate** — use an extinguisher only for a small, contained fire; otherwise evacuate by the nearest safe route.

*PASS — using a fire extinguisher:*
1. **Pull** the safety pin at the top.
2. **Aim** the nozzle low, at the base of the fire.
3. **Squeeze** the handle to release the agent.
4. **Sweep** side to side until the fire is fully out.

**Page 25 — Fire alarm equipment.** Smoke detector · Heat detector · Call point (manual pull station).

**Page 26 — Firefighting and evacuation equipment.** Fire extinguishers · Fire blanket · Sprinkler system · Evacuation routes · Evacuation chairs · Evacuation mattress · Baby mover / apron · Assembly points.

**Page 27 — Match the extinguisher agent to the fire.**

| Agent | Typical use |
|---|---|
| CO₂ | Electrical fires |
| ABC powder | General-purpose |
| Water spray | Wood & paper |
| Foam spray | Flammable liquids |
| Wet chemical | Cooking-oil fires |

**Page 28 — Extinguisher classes.** "Using the wrong extinguisher class can make a fire worse — check the label before you pull the pin."

| Class | Fire type |
|---|---|
| A | Wood, paper, fabric, trash |
| B | Flammable liquids & gases |
| C | Energised electrical equipment |
| D | Combustible or burning metals |
| K | Cooking oils & fats |

**Page 29 — Types of evacuation.**
- **Horizontal** — move patients and staff through fire/smoke doors to an adjacent safe compartment on the same floor. The first response for most fires.
- **Vertical** — move down to a lower floor or ground level using **stairwells, never elevators**, when horizontal evacuation is not sufficient.
- **Full evacuation** — evacuate the entire building to the outdoor assembly point; reserved for the most severe, facility-wide emergencies.

**Page 30 — Fire prevention: what not to bring or use.** Candles & open flame · Unauthorised welding · Unauthorised extensions · Heaters & electrical boilers · Indoor smoking & vaping · Unauthorised microwaves · Uncontrolled flammables.

**Page 31 — Important clarification: oxygen is not flammable, it is an oxidiser.** It does not burn on its own, but it dramatically accelerates combustion. The risk is intensification, not ignition. Keep oxygen cylinders and lines away from open flame, sparks and smoking materials.

### Module 5 — Hazardous Materials & Waste Management (pages 32–41)

**Page 32 — Module cover.** Objective: how chemicals are ordered, labelled, stored and handled; how to respond to Code Orange; how to segregate medical waste.

**Page 33 — Chemical ordering and inventory.**
- Chemicals are ordered **only** through the approved procurement process — no personal or unlogged purchases of hazardous substances.
- Every chemical in your department must appear on the hazardous-materials inventory with its **Safety Data Sheet (SDS)** accessible.
- GHS pictograms on every container show the hazard class at a glance. **Never accept an unlabelled container.**

**Page 34 — GHS pictograms.** Format: black symbol on white background, framed by a red diamond. Nine pictograms, each with a hazard name:

| Pictogram | Hazard name | Pictogram | Hazard name |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skull & crossbones | Acute toxicity | Exploding bomb | Explosives |
| Corrosion | Skin & eye damage | Flame | Flammable |
| Exclamation mark | Irritant | Health hazard | Carcinogen / toxicity |
| Gas cylinder | Gases under pressure | Flame over circle | Oxidisers |
| Environment | Aquatic toxicity | | |

**Page 35 — Reading the NFPA 704 diamond.** Each coloured section rates a hazard from 0 (minimal) to 4 (severe):
- **Blue — Health** (0 = no hazard, 4 = deadly)
- **Red — Flammability** (0 = will not burn, 4 = extremely flammable)
- **Yellow — Reactivity** (0 = stable, 4 = may detonate)
- **White — Special** (symbols such as OX oxidiser, W avoid water)

**Page 36 — NFPA rating scale.** Higher numbers mean greater hazard. Always check the diamond before you open, mix or store a chemical. Examples in the white section: OX (oxidiser), W (avoid water), COR (corrosive), ALK (alkaline).

**Page 37 — Storage, handling and transport.**
- **Storage** — per the SDS: segregated from incompatible substances, secured, away from heat sources.
- **Transport, loading, unloading** — designated carts and routes; never carry incompatible chemicals together; secure containers before moving them.
- **Handling** — wear the PPE specified on the SDS before opening, mixing or dispensing.

**Page 38 — Safety cabinets and SDS access.**
- **Flammable storage cabinets** — double-walled welded steel, adjustable shelves, self-latching door. Flammable liquids belong in an approved cabinet, never on an open shelf.
- **SDS at point of use** — an SDS pocket or binder at or near the cabinet; every SDS reachable in seconds.

**Page 39 — Eyewash and emergency showers.**
- Combination eyewash/shower units are staged near chemical storage and use areas. **Locate the nearest one in your department today**, not during an emergency.
- For chemical contact with eyes or skin: **flush for at least 15 minutes**, continuously, and seek medical evaluation. Do not stop early even if irritation seems to ease.

**Page 40 — Spill response and Code Orange.**
- *Where to find spill equipment:* staged in or near every area that stores hazardous chemicals. Equipment: chemical spill kits (absorbents, neutralisers, PPE), biological spill kits (blood/body fluids), eyewash stations, safety showers.
- *When to activate Code Orange:* a spill of **500 mL or more**, or any highly toxic, reactive or explosive substance. Otherwise contact your cleaning supervisor.
- *Steps:* 1) isolate the area and warn nearby staff; 2) put on the PPE specified on the SDS; 3) contain using the correct spill kit (chemical or biological); 4) report to your supervisor and Safety Services.
- ⚠️ **Inconsistency to resolve:** the Health & Safety 2 deck (see file 02) uses a different threshold narrative ("less than 1 litre … more than 500 mL" for Code Orange). Pick one threshold before publishing.

**Page 41 — Medical waste segregation.**
- **General waste** — non-hazardous, non-infectious everyday trash.
- **Infectious / biohazard** — bags, dressings and materials contaminated with body fluids; labelled biohazard bags.
- **Sharps** — needles, blades and any sharp item into a rigid, puncture-resistant sharps container only. **Never recap.**
- **Body fluid spills** — blood, urine or vomit: contain the area, disinfect per protocol with correct PPE, dispose as biohazard waste.
- Segregation colours vary by facility — follow labelled bins and signage in your department.

### Module 7 — Utility Plan (pages 45–47)

**Page 45 — Module cover.** Objective: essential utility systems and how backup systems work. Keywords: Electrical · Backup Power · Water · IT · HVAC.

**Page 46 — Electrical systems and power.**
- **Standard vs emergency sockets** — emergency (red) sockets are backed by generator power. Know which outlets in your area are which.
- **UPS & generators** — the Uninterruptible Power Supply covers critical equipment instantly; generators take over within seconds during a full outage.
- **Authorised extensions only** — unauthorised extensions are a fire risk and are prohibited.

**Page 47 — Water, IT and HVAC.**
- **Water sources & RO plant** — potable water is treated by Reverse Osmosis (RO); know the location of manual shut-off valves.
- **Sewage systems** — report blockages or backups immediately to Maintenance (contamination risk).
- **IT systems** — clinical systems run on redundant servers; report any network outage to IT immediately, especially in patient-care areas.
- **HVAC** — heating, ventilation and air conditioning maintain air pressure and infection-control zones. **Never disable vents in isolation rooms.**

### Module 8 — Biomedical Plan (pages 48–52)

**Page 48 — Module cover.** Objective: equipment labelling and maintenance, the oxygen network, how to report a problem. Keywords: Equipment Labels · PPM & Inspection · Oxygen Network.

**Page 49 — Equipment labelling and inspection.**
- **Inspection labels** — every biomedical device shows its last inspection date and status. Never use equipment past its due date.
- **Planned Preventive Maintenance (PPM)** — serviced on a fixed schedule by Biomedical Engineering. Report anything overdue or missing its label.
- **Out-of-service tagging** — faulty equipment is tagged "Out of Service" and removed from use immediately. Never attempt your own repair.

**Page 50 — Never relocate medical equipment on your own.** Each approved location is assessed for the equipment placed there. Moving equipment without authorisation (especially radiation and laser equipment, see Module 9) bypasses that assessment. Coordinate with Biomedical Engineering before any move, even temporary.

**Page 51 — Oxygen cylinders and network.**
- **Piped oxygen network** — central oxygen supplies patient areas through wall outlets. Know your zone's emergency shut-off valve.
- **Cylinder storage** — store upright, secured with a chain or stand; clearly separate "full" from "empty."
- **Keep ignition sources away** — oxygen is an oxidiser; never store or use near open flame, sparks or smoking materials (see Module 4).

**Page 52 — Everyone's role: spot it, report it.** If equipment looks damaged or its inspection label is missing or expired, stop using it. Notify Biomedical Engineering immediately; do not attempt repair or bypass a fault.

### Module 9 — Radiation & Laser Safety (pages 53–64)

**Page 53 — Module cover.** Objective: ionising vs non-ionising radiation, ALARA and dose limits, laser classes and hazards, and who to contact. Keywords: Ionising & Non-Ionising Radiation · ALARA · Dose Limits · Laser Safety.

**Page 54 — What is radiation? Ionising vs non-ionising.**
- Radiation = emission or transmission of energy as waves or particles through space or a material.
- **Ionising** — enough energy to remove electrons from atoms; needs dosimeters, shielding and dose tracking. Examples: X-rays, CT.
- **Non-ionising** — lower energy, does not ionise atoms, but still needs precautions (different ones). Examples: MRI, ultrasound, laser light.

**Page 55 — High-risk areas and precautions.**
- **MRI safety** — the magnet is always on; it is a non-ionising *magnetic* hazard. Metal objects, oxygen cylinders and unscreened staff must never enter the MRI room. Screening applies every time.
- **X-ray and imaging areas (ionising)** — lead aprons, shielding and posted warning signs are mandatory whenever imaging equipment is in use. Stand behind the shield or leave the room.

**Page 56 — Principles of radiation protection.**
1. **Justification** — any activity exposing people to radiation must have benefits that clearly justify the risk.
2. **Optimisation** — keep exposure to the minimum reasonable level (the ALARA principle in practice).
3. **Limitation** — a firm upper dose limit applies to every worker and member of the public, outside medical exposures.

**Page 57 — What is ALARA? (As Low As Reasonably Achievable)**
- **Time** — minimise time near a source; less time, less cumulative dose.
- **Distance** — intensity drops sharply with distance; step back when close proximity is not needed.
- **Shielding** — lead aprons, barriers and walls absorb radiation before it reaches you.

**Page 58 — Staying accountable.**
- **Personal dosimeters (OSL)** — staff in or near radiation areas wear an Optically Stimulated Luminescence badge at all times.
- **Lead apron** — required PPE whenever you are in the room during exposure, even for a quick scan.
- **Radiation Safety Officer (RSO)** — oversees compliance and exposure records; your point of contact for any radiation-safety concern.

**Page 59 — Dose limits.**
- Personal dose should never exceed **2 mSv in any 3-month period, or 20 mSv in a year.**

| Level | Value | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Reading level | 1.25 mSv | No action — expected background exposure |
| Notification level | 1.25 – 3.75 mSv | Inform the employee and place them under monitoring |
| Investigation level | Above 3.75 mSv | Follow the facility's over-exposure investigation policy |

**Page 60 — What is a laser?** Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. Laser light is a form of non-ionising radiation but carries its own distinct hazards.

**Page 61 — Laser classification.** Higher class = greater hazard. Check the label before working near any laser device.

| Class | Hazard as described on the slide |
|---|---|
| 1 & 1M | Safe under normal use conditions |
| 2 & 2M | Low power — the blink reflex offers some protection |
| 3 | Moderate power — direct viewing is hazardous |
| 3R | Low risk if safety precautions are followed |
| 3B | Hazardous — direct and reflected viewing |
| 4 | Severe hazard — eyes, skin and fire risk |

**Page 62 — What can go wrong: laser hazards.**
- **Eyes** — permanent damage including cataracts, often without immediate pain.
- **Skin** — burns from direct or reflected beams, especially higher classes.
- **Electrical** — high-voltage components inside laser equipment are a separate and potentially lethal hazard.

**Page 63 — Common sources of laser exposure.** Accidental eye exposure · Misaligned laser beam · Lack of eye protection · Equipment malfunction.

**Page 64 — Key laser safety measures.** Eye protection · Beam management · Control reflections · Environmental safety · Training.
